The whole story in 30 seconds

Four sentences. Everything below is the evidence for them — read as much or as little as you need.

1

Excited and nervous are not opposites. A country — or a person — can be high on both. India is.

2

One overall mood drives most answers. Countries that like AI like almost everything about it, all at once.

3

People know AI better than in 2023, but expect less magic. Familiarity is up; excitement and big promises are down.

4

Trust decides. Suspect the AI is being steered by advertisers, and support collapses. Being told AI is in use? Everyone wants that.

02 — Why so many correlations?

It’s mostly one feeling,
measured 38 ways.

The survey asked 38 questions. When a country feels positive about AI, it scores high on benefits, trust, comfort and excitement together. So the strongest pairs below aren’t 38 separate discoveries — they’re the same overall mood showing up again and again.

In plain English: every variable below is a survey answer turned into a % for each country — e.g. “Excited” is the % who say AI products make them excited. The “×” means “paired with”, and each pair is spelled out in full under its short name, so you never have to guess what a label stands for. A score near 1.00 means the two answers rise and fall together almost perfectly. Near 0 means no link. A negative score means one rises as the other falls.
How to read the correlation score (r) — one scale, from opposite to together:

03 — The stress test: what survives?

Hold the mood constant.
Most links vanish. Five don’t.

Here’s the catch from the last section: if one overall mood inflates almost every score, some “links” are just that mood in disguise. So the analysts re-ran each pair with the overall mood removed (that’s a “partial correlation”). Left = the link is still there on its own. Right = it was the mood all along.

In plain English: imagine two things both look popular only because the whole country is upbeat. Strip out the upbeat-ness, and ask: are they still connected? “Raw” is the score before stripping it out; “Net” is the score after.

Nervousness? Unpredictable.
The best model explains only 13% of it — nothing in the survey reliably predicts which country will be nervous. Nervousness is driven by things outside this deck.
⚠️
Don’t read cause and effect into this. Feeling excited (% who say AI makes them excited) and seeing benefits (% who say AI’s benefits outweigh its drawbacks) track each other almost perfectly (R² = 0.936) — but they’re so tangled up with life already changed (% who say AI has already changed their life) and knowing AI types (% who know which products and services use AI) — statisticians call this collinearity, VIF up to 25 — that country-level data can’t say which one causes which. They rise together; that’s all we know.

Words used on this page

The jargon, in plain English

Nothing above needs these words — but they’re in the source report, so here’s what each one actually means.

Correlation (written “r”)

How strongly two answers move together across countries. +1 = perfectly together, 0 = no link, −1 = perfect opposites. It shows a pattern — it never proves one thing causes the other.

Indicator

Just one survey question turned into a number — e.g. “% who feel excited”. The deck has 38 of them.

The shorthand names used in the charts

Benefits = % who say AI’s benefits outweigh its drawbacks. Trust-less (ads) = % who would trust AI less if advertisers influence it. Life next / life past = % who say AI will change their life in the next 3–5 years / has already changed it. Know types = % who know which products and services use AI. Each chart also spells its pair out in full, right under the short name.

Factor / “general positivity”

One underlying mood that pushes many answers up or down together. Like a tide lifting every boat in the harbour at once.

Partial correlation (“Net”)

The link between two answers after the general mood has been removed — the test of whether a link is real on its own, or just the tide.

Percentage point

The plain difference between two percentages. Nervousness going from 52% to 51.3% is a change of −0.7 percentage points — not “−0.7%”.

Cluster

A group of countries whose whole pattern of answers looks alike, found by the data rather than drawn by hand.

1

Excitement ≠ calm. Nervousness hasn’t fallen in four years, and nothing in the survey predicts it. Plan for it as a permanent feature, not a launch problem.

2

Familiarity, not hype, is what’s growing. Comfort with everyday AI (search, news) is rising fast while big promises lose believers.

3

Trust is the tripwire. Ad influence is the single strongest turn-off; disclosure is expected by 80% everywhere.

C-suite takeaway

Sell usefulness & disclosure.
Not excitement.

In low-positivity Western markets the barrier isn’t ignorance — it’s trust and job anxiety. Transparency is already expected. The win is routine, provable usefulness.

How to read every number here
  • Correlations are across countries (n=32), not people — a country pattern, not proof about any one person
  • With 703 pairs, dozens look “significant” by chance — treat as exploratory
  • Per-country values run 2023–2026, plus 2022 for the benefits question only (the earlier Ipsos wave, 28 countries); gaps are gaps
  • Values are whole %; no per-country margins in these tables
