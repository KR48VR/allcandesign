02 — Why so many correlations?

It’s mostly one feeling,
measured 38 ways.

The survey asked 38 questions. When a country feels positive about AI, it scores high on benefits, trust, comfort and excitement together. So the strongest pairs below aren’t 38 separate discoveries — they’re the same overall mood showing up again and again.

In plain English: every variable below is a survey answer turned into a % for each country — e.g. “Excited” is the % who say AI products make them excited. The “×” means “paired with”, and each pair is spelled out in full under its short name, so you never have to guess what a label stands for. A score near 1.00 means the two answers rise and fall together almost perfectly. Near 0 means no link. A negative score means one rises as the other falls.

03 — The stress test: what survives?

Hold the mood constant.
Most links vanish. Five don’t.

Here’s the catch from the last section: if one overall mood inflates almost every score, some “links” are just that mood in disguise. So the analysts re-ran each pair with the overall mood removed (that’s a “partial correlation”). Left = the link is still there on its own. Right = it was the mood all along.

In plain English: imagine two things both look popular only because the whole country is upbeat. Strip out the upbeat-ness, and ask: are they still connected? “Raw” is the score before stripping it out; “Net” is the score after.

⚠️
Don’t read cause and effect into this. Feeling excited (% who say AI makes them excited) and seeing benefits (% who say AI’s benefits outweigh its drawbacks) track each other almost perfectly (R² = 0.936) — but they’re so tangled up with life already changed (% who say AI has already changed their life) and knowing AI types (% who know which products and services use AI) — statisticians call this collinearity, VIF up to 25 — that country-level data can’t say which one causes which. They rise together; that’s all we know.

Words used on this page

The jargon, in plain English

Nothing above needs these words — but they’re in the source report, so here’s what each one actually means.

Correlation (written “r”)

How strongly two answers move together across countries. +1 = perfectly together, 0 = no link, −1 = perfect opposites. It shows a pattern — it never proves one thing causes the other.

Indicator

Just one survey question turned into a number — e.g. “% who feel excited”. The deck has 38 of them.

The shorthand names used in the charts

Benefits = % who say AI’s benefits outweigh its drawbacks. Trust-less (ads) = % who would trust AI less if advertisers influence it. Life next / life past = % who say AI will change their life in the next 3–5 years / has already changed it. Know types = % who know which products and services use AI. Each chart also spells its pair out in full, right under the short name.

Factor / “general positivity”

One underlying mood that pushes many answers up or down together. Like a tide lifting every boat in the harbour at once.

Partial correlation (“Net”)

The link between two answers after the general mood has been removed — the test of whether a link is real on its own, or just the tide.

Percentage point

The plain difference between two percentages. Nervousness going from 52% to 51.3% is a change of −0.7 percentage points — not “−0.7%”.

Cluster

A group of countries whose whole pattern of answers looks alike, found by the data rather than drawn by hand.

The finding, in two claims

Two claims. One analogy
runs through both.

Each claim gets the same picture: a class taking a test. The students are countries, the test is the survey’s 38 questions, and “knowing the subject” is feeling positive about AI.

CLAIM 1 — HOW MUCH 79%

“This feeling is the underlying pattern because it explains 79% of the differences in the responses.”

Think of a class test: a class takes a 38-question test. Scores differ from student to student — and most of that difference comes down to one thing: how well each student knows the subject. Here, the students are countries, and the one thing is how positive each country feels about AI.

Where the 79% comes from: factor analysis — the standard check researchers run when lots of survey questions move together. It looks for one underlying pattern across all the questions, and here the first pattern explains about 79% of the variation across all 38 questions. The 79% is a “how much”, not a count — it is not a score out of anything.

CLAIM 2 — THE COUNT 29 of 37

“Of all the questions, the excited question is the best single window into that feeling: 29 of the other 37 questions move tightly with it.”

Same class, same test: now look at just one question on that test — the “excited” question. Know how a student answered that one question, and you can guess how they answered 29 of the other 37. That is why excitement is the best single window into the whole feeling.

Keep the two numbers apart: 79% is a “how much” (claim 1). 29 of 37 is a count of questions (claim 2) — the only count on this tab. For the record, excitement on its own explains about 71% of the differences on average — about 7 out of 10 — or 75% if you set nervousness and disclosure aside.

WHAT FEEDS IT · THE CAUSE SIDE

People think AI helps

What people believe AI delivers accounts for 95% of the differences in excitement between countries (R² = 0.95). One belief on its own — “the benefits outweigh the drawbacks” — accounts for 94% (R² = 0.94).

In plain English: if people can see a real benefit — saves time, helps health, makes work easier — they get excited. No benefit seen, no excitement.

100%?
So why not 100%?
Because the gaps are about the same, not exactly the same — 57, 51, 46, 48, 49, and 32 for “will AI replace my job”. And Canada vs China is the most extreme pair, not the average: the 79% is the average across all 32 countries and all 38 questions. The leftover ~21% is real — nervousness runs the other way, disclosure barely differs, and countries buck the pattern on single questions: Japan, for instance, is middling on excitement at 47 but low on trusting AI companies with data at 30.

How to read this

The two numbers,
in plain English.

r — how tightly two answers move together

+1 means they rise and fall together perfectly across countries, 0 means no link, and a negative number means one rises as the other falls. Excitement and benefits: r = +0.97. Excitement and nervousness: r = −0.16.

R² — how much one answer can explain

R² tells you how much of a question’s differences you can explain from one clue. R² = 0.95 means 95 out of 100 parts of the difference. It is just r multiplied by itself — so r = +0.97 gives r² = 0.94.

Read with care: these are correlations across 32 countries in 2026 — they show things move together, not that one causes the other. With only 32 countries, treat this as exploratory: a clear pattern worth acting on carefully, not a proof. Source: Ipsos AI Monitor 2026; analysis of published Ipsos figures.

Annex — Independent cross-check

Cross-check: Gallup 2026
tells the same story.

A second, independent survey — fielded a few months after Ipsos — found the same ranking: China at the top, Singapore high, the United States near the bottom among rich countries. It does not change anything in the Ipsos findings above. It is a cross-check only: the two datasets are not merged, and no Ipsos number or chart on this page has changed.

In plain English: the questions are not identical, so this is not apples-to-apples. Gallup’s “mostly help” is closest to Ipsos’s “benefits outweigh drawbacks”, and Gallup’s “daily life mostly better” is closest to Ipsos’s life measures. Ipsos’s “excited” is an emotion, not a judgement — it is shown alongside for context, not as the same question.

Where they agree — and what Gallup adds

  • Same ranking: China top in both, Singapore high in both, the US near the bottom in both.
  • Gallup runs hotter — China 93% vs 85%, Singapore 77% vs 68% on the closest-matching question. That is mostly who was asked: Gallup asks only AI-aware adults, while Ipsos asks all adults in its online sample. Ipsos itself notes its samples in 15 countries, including China and Singapore, skew more urban, educated and affluent.
  • New countries: Gallup covers places Ipsos does not — Vietnam, Nigeria and Malta near the top; Bangladesh, Afghanistan and Malawi near the bottom.
  • A worry measure Ipsos does not headline: 74% of AI-aware Americans say AI makes them feel worried — the highest of any Western high-income country in Gallup’s sample.
  • One real divergence — Israel: Gallup 69% “mostly help” vs Ipsos 51% benefits and 39% excited. Different question, different population, different month — do not read a trend into that one country.
